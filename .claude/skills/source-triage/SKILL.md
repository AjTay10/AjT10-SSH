# Source triage

The default assumption for any claim encountered online should be that it is
optimized for spread, not for accuracy. Those are different objectives and
they diverge most on exactly the claims that spread furthest.

## Fast triage: five questions, two minutes

1. **Who benefits if I believe this?** Follow the money before the logic.
   Almost every "study" in a commercial niche was commissioned by a vendor.
2. **What is the primary source?** Not who reported it — who *measured* it.
   If the chain does not terminate in a measurement, it terminates in nothing.
3. **What is the date of the underlying data?** Not the article's publish
   date. A 2026 article routinely cites a 2019 survey.
4. **Is the specific number plausible?** Order-of-magnitude check against
   anything you know. Most viral statistics fail this in ten seconds.
5. **What would this look like if it were false?** If false and true look
   identical from here, the source is not evidence either way.

## Citation laundering

The dominant failure mode of internet research. A number is invented or
estimated, gets repeated by a credible-looking outlet, and every subsequent
citation points at the repeater. Volume of agreement then reads as
corroboration.

**Trace it.** Follow the chain back until you hit a measurement or a dead end.
Landmarks that should trigger a trace:
- A suspiciously round number ("70% of consumers…")
- A statistic with no year attached
- "Studies show" with no study named
- A figure that appears verbatim across many sites — copy a distinctive phrase
  into a search and watch the same sentence appear a hundred times

Famous dead ends exist in every field: numbers everyone cites, that trace to
one unsourced slide. Assume the number in front of you is one of them until
the chain resolves.

## Detecting AI-generated filler

Increasingly what search returns. Not automatically false — but it is
evidence of *what is commonly said*, never of what is true, because it was
generated by averaging what was commonly said.

Tells, in rough order of reliability:
- Perfectly balanced structure: every section the same length, every list
  exactly three or five items
- Fluent, specific-sounding, and unfalsifiable throughout — nothing that
  could be checked
- No named individuals, no dates, no dollar amounts, no proper nouns beyond
  the topic itself
- Enthusiastic hedging: "it's important to note", "in today's landscape"
- Cites no primary source, links only to other overview articles
- Zero first-person experience: no "we tried this and it failed"
- The comment section, if any, is disconnected from the content

A page with a real screenshot, a specific dollar figure, a named person, and
an admission of a failure is worth fifty overview articles.

## Detecting astroturf and engagement farming

For accounts, reviews, and "organic" enthusiasm:
- Account age versus opinion intensity — brand-new accounts with fully
  formed strong positions
- Reviews clustered in time rather than spread across the product's life
- Vocabulary overlap across supposedly independent reviewers
- Engagement shape: high replies with low saves/shares means the post
  provoked, it did not inform. Rage-bait optimizes exactly this ratio
- Followers with no posting history
- The claim's specificity drops sharply when questioned

## Study-quality checks

When the source is an actual study:

| Check | Failure looks like |
|---|---|
| Sample size and who | n=200, all recruited from the vendor's own mailing list |
| Preregistered? | Outcome measure differs from what was clearly intended |
| Who funded it | Vendor-funded studies favor the vendor at well-known rates |
| Effect size, not just p | p<0.05 on a 0.3% lift is statistically real and practically irrelevant |
| Absolute vs relative | "50% reduction" of a 0.002% risk |
| Replication | Single unreplicated result in a field with a replication problem |

Ask for the effect size every time someone reports significance. The two
questions get conflated constantly and only one of them affects a decision.

## Verdict format

Never a bare "credible" or "not credible":

```
CLAIM        "Short-form video gets 2.5x more engagement than static images"
CHAIN        Blog → agency report → agency's own client accounts, n=14, 2023
TIER         D reporting a C source
FUNDER       An agency selling short-form video production
PLAUSIBLE?   Directionally yes; the multiplier is unsupported and platform-
             specific. Engagement is also defined differently for each format,
             which makes the ratio partly definitional rather than empirical.
VERDICT      Usable as a directional hint. Not citable as a number.
BETTER       The user's own last 90 days, normalized per platform — which
             answers the actual question for the actual account.
```

That last line matters: for most marketing claims the user's own data
outranks any external source, and it is sitting in an export they already have.
